§1 Hook

Why classical physics breaks down

In everyday physics, velocities add the way Newton and Galileo expected. If a train moves at 60 mph and a ball is thrown forward at 40 mph, a ground observer measures the ball moving at 100 mph.

$$v_{total}=v_{train}+v_{ball}$$

The problem appears when the ball is replaced with light. Classical physics predicts that a flashlight on a fast train should produce light moving faster than $c$ to someone on the ground. Experiments show this never happens.

Packet answer: A train at 60 mph plus a ball at 40 mph gives 100 mph. A train at $0.5c$ plus a flashlight would classically predict $1.5c$, but the real measured value is always $c=3.00\times10^8\,\text{m/s}$.

Michelson-Morley experiment

Physicists expected light to travel through a medium called the luminiferous aether. If Earth moved through that aether, light should have traveled at slightly different speeds in different directions. Michelson and Morley measured no difference.

Packet answer: The medium was the luminiferous aether. The result was a null result: no directional difference in light speed was detected.
§2 Postulates

Einstein's two postulates

Postulate 1: Principle of Relativity

The laws of physics are the same in all inertial, non-accelerating reference frames.

Inertial frame: a frame that is not accelerating. It may be at rest or moving at constant velocity.

Example: On a smooth airplane with the windows closed, you cannot use a local physics experiment to tell whether you are moving at constant velocity.

Postulate 2: Constant Speed of Light

The speed of light in vacuum is the same for all observers, regardless of the motion of the source or observer.

Even if you run toward a light beam at $0.5c$, you still measure the beam moving at $c$, not $1.5c$.

Deep implication: If every observer must measure the same light speed, then space and time cannot be absolute. Time intervals and lengths must change between observers.
§3 Time Dilation

Moving clocks run slow

A light clock is a thought experiment using two mirrors and a photon bouncing between them. In the clock's own rest frame, the photon moves straight up and down. To an outside observer who sees the clock moving sideways, the photon follows a diagonal path.

At rest, the photon travels the shortest up-down path.

Moving frame: the photon path is longer, but light still moves at c.

$$\Delta t=\gamma\Delta t_0 \qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}$$
SymbolMeaningUnits / Notes
$\Delta t_0$Proper time; time measured in the object's own rest frameseconds; shortest time interval
$\Delta t$Dilated time; time measured by an observer who sees the clock movingseconds; longer than $\Delta t_0$
$v$Relative speed between framesm/s or fraction of $c$
$c$Speed of light$3.00\times10^8\,\text{m/s}$
$\gamma$Lorentz factorunitless; always $\ge 1$

Derivation answer for the packet

Using the light-clock triangle, the photon path is the hypotenuse, the mirror spacing gives the vertical leg, and the clock's sideways motion gives the horizontal leg.

Use the full-tick version shown here. Some textbooks use a half-tick triangle; the final equation is the same.

$$c^2\Delta t^2=c^2\Delta t_0^2+v^2\Delta t^2$$ $$c^2\Delta t^2-v^2\Delta t^2=c^2\Delta t_0^2$$ $$\Delta t^2(c^2-v^2)=c^2\Delta t_0^2$$ $$\Delta t^2\left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)=\Delta t_0^2$$ $$\Delta t=\frac{\Delta t_0}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}=\gamma\Delta t_0$$
Common mistake: $\Delta t_0$ is the time measured by the person traveling with the clock. It is not automatically the Earth time. Identify the clock/event first.

Sample Problem 1: Basic time dilation

Problem: A spaceship travels at $0.80c$. One hour passes on the spaceship. How much time passes for an Earth observer?

Step 1: Identify $\Delta t_0=1.0\,\text{h}$ because this is measured by the spaceship clock.

Step 2: Calculate $\gamma$:

$$\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-0.80^2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{0.36}}=1.667$$

Step 3: Use $\Delta t=\gamma\Delta t_0$:

$$\Delta t=(1.667)(1.0\,h)=1.67\,h$$

Answer: Earth measures about 1.67 hours.

Sample Problem 2: Solve for speed

Problem: A traveler wants 10 years to pass on Earth while only 5 years pass on the ship. What speed is required?

Here $\Delta t=10\,\text{yr}$ and $\Delta t_0=5\,\text{yr}$, so $\gamma=\Delta t/\Delta t_0=2$.

$$2=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}$$

Square both sides:

$$4=\frac{1}{1-v^2/c^2}$$

Invert and solve:

$$1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}=\frac14$$

$$\frac{v^2}{c^2}=\frac34$$

$$\frac{v}{c}=\sqrt{0.75}=0.866$$

Answer: The ship must travel at about $0.866c$.

§4 Reality Check

Experimental proof: muons and GPS

Cosmic muons

Muons are unstable particles created high in Earth's atmosphere. Their rest-frame lifetime is so short that classical physics predicts they should decay before reaching the ground. But they are detected at Earth's surface because time dilation extends their lifetime in Earth's frame.

$$v=0.998c \Rightarrow \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-0.998^2}}\approx15.8$$ $$\Delta t=\gamma\Delta t_0=(15.8)(2.2\,\mu s)\approx34.8\,\mu s$$

Distance traveled:

$$d=v\Delta t=(0.998)(3.00\times10^8)(34.8\times10^{-6})\approx1.04\times10^4\,m\approx10.4\,km$$
Packet answer: The muon can reach Earth because Earth observers measure the muon's lifetime as longer. In the muon's frame, the atmosphere is length-contracted instead. Both explanations agree.

§5 Length Contraction

Moving objects contract along the direction of motion

$$L=\frac{L_0}{\gamma}=L_0\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}}$$
SymbolMeaningKey idea
$L_0$Proper length; measured in the object's rest frameLongest measured length
$L$Contracted length; measured by observer who sees object movingShorter than $L_0$
$\gamma$Lorentz factorGreater speed means larger $\gamma$ and stronger contraction
Packet answer: Contraction only occurs along the direction of motion. Dimensions perpendicular to motion are unchanged.

The object itself is not “squished” in its own frame; different observers measure different lengths.

Sample Problem 1: Rocket length

Problem: A rocket has proper length $L_0=500\,m$ and travels at $0.80c$. What length does a ground observer measure?

Calculate $\gamma$:

$$\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-0.80^2}}=1.667$$

Use length contraction:

$$L=\frac{L_0}{\gamma}=\frac{500\,m}{1.667}=300\,m$$

Answer: The ground observer measures the rocket as 300 m long.

Sample Problem 2: Contraction at high speed

Problem: A 100 m spaceship is measured to be only 60 m long by an outside observer. What is $\gamma$ and what speed is the ship traveling?

Start with $L=L_0/\gamma$:

$$60=\frac{100}{\gamma} \Rightarrow \gamma=\frac{100}{60}=1.667$$

Now solve for speed:

$$1.667=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}$$

This is the same $\gamma$ as $0.80c$, so:

$$v=0.80c$$

Answer: $\gamma=1.667$ and the ship travels at $0.80c$.

Sample Problem 3: Muon atmosphere length

Problem: In Earth's frame, the atmosphere is about 10 km thick. A muon travels with $\gamma=15.8$. What thickness does the muon measure?

Use $L=L_0/\gamma$:

$$L=\frac{10,000\,m}{15.8}=633\,m$$

Answer: In the muon's frame, the atmosphere is only about 630 m thick.

§5 Simultaneity

There is no universal “now”

Imagine lightning strikes both ends of a moving train simultaneously in the ground frame. The ground observer stands at the midpoint between the strikes, so both flashes travel equal distances and arrive together.

The train observer is at the center of the moving train. While the light travels, the train observer moves toward the front flash and away from the rear flash. Since light speed is still $c$ in both directions, the front flash reaches the train observer first.

In the ground frame the strikes are simultaneous; in the train frame the front flash arrives first.

Packet answer: Ground observer: both flashes arrive at the same time. Train observer: the front flash arrives first. Events simultaneous in one frame are not necessarily simultaneous in another.
§6 Energy & Mass

Energy and mass are equivalent

Classical kinetic energy works only at low speeds:

$$KE=\frac12mv^2 \qquad \text{valid when } v\ll c$$

Relativistic kinetic energy is:

$$KE_{rel}=(\gamma-1)mc^2$$

Einstein's most famous equation is:

$$E_{rest}=mc^2$$
Packet answer: Mass is energy. Energy has inertia. Since $c^2\approx9\times10^{16}\,J/kg$, a tiny amount of mass stores an enormous amount of energy.

One gram example

$$E=mc^2=(0.001\,kg)(3.00\times10^8\,m/s)^2=9.0\times10^{13}\,J$$

Energy-momentum relation

$$E^2=(pc)^2+(mc^2)^2$$
  • Massive particle at rest: $p=0$, so $E=mc^2$.
  • Photon: $m=0$, so $E=pc=hf$.
  • Low-speed limit: $E\approx mc^2+\frac12mv^2$.
§7–8 General Relativity

Gravity is spacetime curvature

Newton's gravityEinstein's gravity
Gravity is a force between masses.Gravity is the curvature of spacetime.
Acts instantaneously in the old model.Changes in gravity propagate at the speed of light.
Space and time are fixed backgrounds.Space and time are dynamic and can curve.
Works well for everyday scales.Needed for black holes, GPS, light bending, gravitational waves.
$$G_{\mu\nu}=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}$$
Packet answer: Mass tells spacetime how to curve; spacetime tells mass how to move. Free-falling objects follow geodesics, the straightest possible paths through curved spacetime.

The sheet analogy is useful, but real spacetime is 4D and time curvature matters.

Geodesics

  • On a flat surface, the shortest path is a straight line.
  • On a sphere, the shortest path is a great circle.
  • In curved spacetime, free-falling objects follow geodesics.
Important wording: Earth orbits the Sun because Earth follows a geodesic through the Sun's curved spacetime. You feel gravity because the ground stops you from following your natural free-fall path.
§9 Black Holes

Black holes and extreme spacetime

A black hole forms when mass is compressed so much that spacetime curves strongly enough that nothing, not even light, can escape.

$$v_{esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$$ $$r_s=\frac{2GM}{c^2}$$

A black hole has no solid surface at the event horizon; it is a boundary in spacetime.

RegionMeaning
SingularityWhere curvature tends toward infinity and current physics breaks down.
Event horizonPoint of no return; located at $r=r_s$.
Photon sphereRegion near $1.5r_s$ where light can orbit unstably.
Accretion diskSuperheated gas spiraling inward.

Gravitational time dilation

$$\frac{\tau}{t}=\sqrt{1-\frac{r_s}{r}}$$
  • At $r=2r_s$: $\tau/t=\sqrt{1-1/2}=0.707$.
  • At $r=4r_s$: $\tau/t=\sqrt{1-1/4}=0.866$.
  • Far away: $\tau/t\rightarrow1$.
  • At the event horizon: $\tau/t\rightarrow0$ for a distant observer.

Tidal forces

Spaghettification happens because gravity is stronger on the side of an object closer to the black hole than on the side farther away. This stretches the object radially and squeezes it laterally.

$$F_{tidal}\propto\frac{GMr}{R^3}$$

Hawking radiation

Hawking radiation is the theoretical prediction that black holes can slowly radiate energy because of quantum effects near the event horizon. It has not been directly confirmed experimentally for real black holes because the temperature is incredibly tiny.

$$T_H=\frac{\hbar c^3}{8\pi GMk_B}$$

Light bending

General relativity predicts that light follows curved geodesics near massive objects. Einstein's prediction is twice the Newtonian prediction because GR includes curvature of both space and time.

$$\delta\phi_{GR}=\frac{4GM}{c^2b}$$
§10 Summary

Four key ideas students should leave with

  1. The speed of light is absolute: all observers measure $c$.
  2. Time and length are relative: moving clocks run slow and moving objects contract along the direction of motion.
  3. Mass and energy are equivalent: $E=mc^2$.
  4. Gravity is spacetime curvature: objects follow geodesics through curved spacetime.
Final thought: Astronauts are technically time travelers toward the future. The effect is tiny at ISS speeds, but it becomes large near light speed or near strong gravity.
