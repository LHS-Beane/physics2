Core Notes

1. What is electric charge?

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter. There are two types of charge: positive and negative.

  • Like charges repel.
  • Opposite charges attract.
  • Charge is conserved.
  • Charge is quantized in integer multiples of the elementary charge.
q = Ne     where e = 1.60 × 10−19 C
Most everyday objects are neutral because they contain equal amounts of positive and negative charge.
2. Conductors, insulators, and polarization

Conductors allow charge to move easily. Insulators do not. A neutral object can still be attracted to a charged object because its charges can shift slightly, producing polarization.

A charged object can attract a neutral object without the neutral object having net charge.
3. Coulomb’s law

Coulomb’s law gives the electric force between two point charges.

F = k |q1q2| / r2
k = 8.99 × 109 N·m2/C2

The force acts along the line joining the two charges.

  • If the charges have the same sign, the force is repulsive.
  • If the charges have opposite signs, the force is attractive.
Coulomb’s law gives the magnitude. You must determine the direction from the physical situation.
4. Vector review

Electric force and electric field must often be written in component form.

\vec{A} = Ax \hat{i} + Ay \hat{j}
|\vec{A}| = √(Ax2 + Ay2)
\theta = tan−1(Ay/Ax)
Never trust the calculator alone for angle direction. Always identify the correct quadrant from the sketch.
5. Superposition

When more than one charge is present, the total force or field is the vector sum of contributions from each charge.

\vec{F}net = Σ\vec{F}i
\vec{E}net = Σ\vec{E}i

Strategy:

  1. Draw the geometry clearly.
  2. Determine the direction of each contribution before calculating.
  3. Find the magnitude of each force or field.
  4. Break vectors into components.
  5. Add components.
  6. Convert back to magnitude and direction if needed.
6. Electric field

Instead of repeatedly calculating the force on many different charges, we define an electric field created by the source charge.

\vec{E} = \vec{F}/q

Electric field is the force per unit positive test charge.

Units of electric field: N/C
The electric field exists whether or not a test charge is present. The test charge only measures the field.
7. Electric field of a point charge
\vec{E} = kq/r2 \; \hat{r}
  • For a positive source charge, the field points away from the charge.
  • For a negative source charge, the field points toward the charge.
8. Transition to continuous charge

Real objects are often not isolated point charges. Charge may be spread out along a line, over a surface, or throughout a volume.

\vec{E} = Σ\vec{E}i    becomes    \vec{E} = ∫ d\vec{E}

This is where calculus enters naturally. We build the total field by adding infinitely many tiny contributions.

Calculus Refresher

You do not need advanced integration techniques this week, but you do need to understand what an integral means physically.

  • A sum of many tiny pieces
  • A way to model a continuous distribution
  • A replacement for Σ when the pieces become infinitesimally small
dq = λ dx

For a uniformly charged rod, line charge density λ tells you how much charge exists per meter.

Common Symbols

  • q = charge
  • r = distance
  • F = force
  • E = electric field
  • k = Coulomb constant
  • λ = line charge density
  • dq = a tiny piece of charge

Worked Example 1 — Force Between Two Charges

Problem

A +3.0 μC charge is located at x = 0.00 m. A −2.0 μC charge is located at x = 0.40 m. Find the electric force on the −2.0 μC charge.

Solution

  1. The charges have opposite signs, so the force is attractive.
  2. The force on the negative charge points toward the positive charge, which is to the left.
  3. Use Coulomb’s law for magnitude.
F = k |q1q2| / r2
F = (8.99 × 109) (3.0 × 10−6)(2.0 × 10−6) / (0.40)2
F = 0.337 N
Final answer: 0.337 N to the left.

Worked Example 2 — Net Electric Field from Two Charges

Problem

A +3.0 μC charge is at (0.40 m, 0). A −2.0 μC charge is at (0, 0.30 m). Find the net electric field at the origin.

Solution Outline

  1. Find the field due to each charge separately.
  2. Determine directions first.
  3. Add x- and y-components.

Field from +3.0 μC: At the origin, field points away from the positive charge, so it points left.

E1 = k|q|/r2 = (8.99 × 109)(3.0 × 10−6)/(0.40)2 = 1.69 × 105 N/C
\vec{E}1 = (−1.69 × 105)\hat{i}

Field from −2.0 μC: At the origin, field points toward the negative charge, so it points upward.

E2 = (8.99 × 109)(2.0 × 10−6)/(0.30)2 = 2.00 × 105 N/C
\vec{E}2 = (2.00 × 105)\hat{j}
\vec{E}net = (−1.69 × 105)\hat{i} + (2.00 × 105)\hat{j}
|\vec{E}| = 2.62 × 105 N/C
Final answer: \(\vec{E}_{net} = (−1.69 × 105)\hat{i} + (2.00 × 105)\hat{j}\) N/C, magnitude 2.62 × 105 N/C, direction about 130° from +x.

Worked Example 3 — Why Continuous Charge Requires Integration

Problem

A rod of length L lies on the x-axis and carries uniform charge Q. Explain how you would begin finding the electric field at a point above the rod.

Key Setup

  1. Break the rod into tiny pieces of length dx.
  2. Assign each piece a charge dq.
  3. Find the field dE from each small piece.
  4. Add all contributions using an integral.
λ = Q/L
dq = λ dx
\vec{E} = ∫ d\vec{E}
This week, the goal is understanding the setup. Full continuous-charge derivations come next.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting to convert μC to C.
  • Using r instead of r2 in Coulomb’s law.
  • Ignoring direction and sign.
  • Adding field magnitudes when vectors point in different directions.
  • Using the wrong quadrant for the final angle.
  • Treating electric field as something caused by the test charge.
