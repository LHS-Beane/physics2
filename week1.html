Core Notes

1. Electric charge

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter. There are two kinds of electric charge: positive and negative.

  • Like charges repel.
  • Opposite charges attract.
  • Charge is conserved.
  • Charge is quantized.
q = Ne
e = 1.60 × 10−19 C
An object appears neutral when its total positive charge equals its total negative charge.
2. Conductors, insulators, and polarization

Conductors allow charge to move easily. Insulators do not allow charge to move freely through the material.

A neutral object can still be attracted to a charged object because its internal charges shift slightly. This is called polarization.

A charged object can attract a neutral object because opposite charges become slightly closer than like charges after polarization.
3. Coulomb’s law

Coulomb’s law gives the magnitude of the electric force between two point charges.

F = k|q₁q₂| / r²
k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²

To get the full physics answer, determine direction from the charge signs and the geometry.

  • Like charges → repulsion
  • Opposite charges → attraction
4. Vector review

Electric force and electric field are vectors. In two dimensions, it is often easiest to work in components.

A⃗ = Aₓî + Aᵧĵ
|A⃗| = √(Aₓ² + Aᵧ²)
θ = tan⁻¹(Aᵧ / Aₓ)
The calculator only gives a reference angle. You must use the sketch to identify the correct quadrant.
5. Superposition

When more than one charge is present, the total force or field is the vector sum of the contributions from each individual charge.

F⃗net = ΣF⃗ᵢ
E⃗net = ΣE⃗ᵢ
  1. Draw the geometry.
  2. Determine direction first.
  3. Compute magnitudes.
  4. Break vectors into components.
  5. Add components.
  6. Convert back to magnitude and direction if needed.
6. Electric field

Electric field tells you how a source charge changes the space around it. It is defined as force per unit positive test charge.

E⃗ = F⃗ / q
Units of electric field: N/C
The field exists even if no test charge is present. The test charge only measures the field.
7. Electric field of a point charge
E⃗ = kq / r² · r̂
  • Positive source charge → field points away
  • Negative source charge → field points toward
8. Continuous charge and the need for calculus

When charge is spread out, we cannot just add a few separate contributions. Instead, we treat the charge as many tiny pieces and integrate.

E⃗ = ΣE⃗ᵢ → E⃗ = ∫ dE⃗
dq = λ dx

Here, λ is line charge density, or charge per unit length.

Worked Example 1 — Force Between Two Charges

Problem

A +3.0 μC charge is located at x = 0.00 m. A −2.0 μC charge is located at x = 0.40 m. Find the electric force on the −2.0 μC charge.

  1. The charges have opposite signs, so the force is attractive.
  2. The force on the negative charge points toward the positive charge, so it points left.
  3. Use Coulomb’s law for the magnitude.
F = k|q₁q₂| / r²
F = (8.99 × 10⁹)(3.0 × 10⁻⁶)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶) / (0.40)²
F = 0.337 N
Final answer: 0.337 N to the left.

Worked Example 2 — Net Electric Field at the Origin

Problem

A +3.0 μC charge is at (0.40 m, 0). A −2.0 μC charge is at (0, 0.30 m). Find the net electric field at the origin.

Field from +3.0 μC: At the origin, the field points away from the positive charge, so it points left.

E₁ = (8.99 × 10⁹)(3.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.40)² = 1.69 × 10⁵ N/C
E⃗₁ = (−1.69 × 10⁵)î

Field from −2.0 μC: At the origin, the field points toward the negative charge, so it points upward.

E₂ = (8.99 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.30)² = 2.00 × 10⁵ N/C
E⃗₂ = (2.00 × 10⁵)ĵ
E⃗net = (−1.69 × 10⁵)î + (2.00 × 10⁵)ĵ
|E⃗net| = √[(−1.69 × 10⁵)² + (2.00 × 10⁵)²] = 2.62 × 10⁵ N/C
Final answer: E⃗net = (−1.69 × 10⁵)î + (2.00 × 10⁵)ĵ N/C, magnitude 2.62 × 10⁵ N/C, direction about 130° from +x.

Worked Example 3 — Transition to Continuous Charge

Problem

A rod of length L lies on the x-axis and carries uniform charge Q. Explain how to begin finding the electric field at a point above the rod.

  1. Break the rod into tiny pieces of length dx.
  2. Assign each piece a small charge dq.
  3. Write the field contribution from one tiny piece.
  4. Add all contributions with an integral.
λ = Q / L
dq = λ dx
E⃗ = ∫ dE⃗
The main goal right now is the setup. Full derivations of continuous distributions become central very soon.

Practice with Collapsible Answer Reveals

Work each problem first. Only open the answer after you have made a serious attempt.

Practice 1

Find the electric field 0.20 m to the right of a charge of −6.0 μC.

Reveal answer
E = (8.99 × 10⁹)(6.0 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.20)² = 1.35 × 10⁶ N/C

Because the source charge is negative, the field points toward the charge. At a point to the right of the charge, that means the field points left.

Answer: 1.35 × 10⁶ N/C to the left.
Practice 2

Two +5.0 μC charges are placed 1.0 m apart. Find the electric field at the midpoint.

Reveal answer

Each positive charge creates a field at the midpoint pointing away from itself. These two fields have equal magnitude and opposite direction, so they cancel.

Answer: 0 N/C.
Practice 3

Explain why electric field is a vector instead of a scalar.

Reveal answer

Electric field must describe both how strongly a positive test charge would be pushed or pulled and the direction of that push or pull. Since direction is essential, electric field must be a vector quantity.

Week 1 Review Checklist

  • I can use Coulomb’s law with correct SI units.
  • I can identify electric force and field directions from a sketch.
  • I can add vectors in component form.
  • I can define electric field in words and with an equation.
  • I can explain why integration appears in electrostatics.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting to convert μC into C.
  • Using r instead of r² in Coulomb’s law.
  • Ignoring direction entirely.
  • Adding vector magnitudes when directions differ.
  • Letting the calculator choose the quadrant for you.
  • Thinking the test charge creates the electric field.
