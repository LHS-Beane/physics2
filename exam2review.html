What this exam review covers

This review is centered on three major units: microscopic current and resistance, resistor networks and Kirchhoff analysis, and magnetic fields and magnetic forces. The goal is not just answer recognition. Students should be able to determine the correct model, choose an equation, apply sign conventions, and explain the physical reasoning.

Resistance + Current

Drift velocity, resistivity, current density, electric field in a wire, power, temperature effects.

DC Circuits

Series/parallel reduction, internal resistance, equivalent resistance, Kirchhoff loop and junction rules.

Magnetism

Right-hand rules, forces on charges and currents, long-wire fields, Ampère's law, solenoids, induction.

Target Skills

  • Translate a physical situation into a circuit or field model.
  • Predict directions before calculating magnitudes.
  • Use proportional reasoning for ranking questions.
  • Work symbolically before substituting numbers.
  • Check units at every stage.
  • Distinguish between field, force, current, and potential difference.

Core formula sheet

These are the equations students should know how to use, interpret, and connect conceptually.

Current and Drift

$I = \dfrac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}$

$I = nqAv_d$

$J = \dfrac{I}{A} = nqv_d$

Narrower wire means larger drift speed for the same current.

Resistance and Resistivity

$R = \rho \dfrac{L}{A}$

$\rho(T) = \rho_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]$

$A = \pi r^2$

Resistivity is a material property. Resistance depends on both material and geometry.

Microscopic Ohm's Law

$V = IR$

$E = \rho J$

$\Delta V = EL$

Inside a resistor, the electric field drives the current density.

Power

$P = IV$

$P = I^2R$

$P = \dfrac{V^2}{R}$

$E_{\text{thermal}} = Pt$

Equivalent Resistance

Series: $R_{eq}=R_1+R_2+\cdots$

Parallel: $\dfrac{1}{R_{eq}}=\dfrac{1}{R_1}+\dfrac{1}{R_2}+\cdots$

Series → same current. Parallel → same voltage.

Kirchhoff Rules

Junction: $\sum I_{in}=\sum I_{out}$

Loop: $\sum \Delta V = 0$

Be consistent with current directions and battery/resistor signs.

Magnetic Force

$\vec{F}=q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$

$F=qvB\sin\theta$

$\vec{F}=I\vec{L}\times\vec{B}$

A negative charge reverses the right-hand-rule result.

Fields from Currents

$B=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$

$B_{solenoid}=\mu_0 n I$

$\oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{\ell}=\mu_0 I_{enc}$

Induction

$\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta$

$\mathcal{E}=\left|\dfrac{\Delta \Phi_B}{\Delta t}\right|$

$\mathcal{E}=N\left|\dfrac{\Delta \Phi_B}{\Delta t}\right|$

Lenz's law determines the sign and direction.

Topic review

Click a topic to switch the focused review panel.

Resistance + Current

Big ideas

  • For steady current, the same current passes through every cross section of a single series path.
  • Drift speed is typically tiny even though the electric field signal is established quickly.
  • Changing length or area changes resistance geometrically; changing material changes resistivity.
  • Microscopic and macroscopic Ohm's law tell the same story from different scales.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking electrons move near the speed of light in ordinary wires.
  • Confusing resistance with resistivity.
  • Forgetting area scales with radius squared.
  • Using $P=V/R$ instead of one of the correct power relations.

DC Circuits + Kirchhoff

Big ideas

  • Series branches share current; parallel branches share potential difference.
  • Equivalent resistance simplifies structure before detailed current calculations.
  • Kirchhoff's junction rule is charge conservation.
  • Kirchhoff's loop rule is energy conservation.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up resistor drops and battery rises when tracing a loop.
  • Combining resistors that are not truly in simple series or parallel.
  • Assuming current splits equally even when branch resistances differ.
  • Ignoring internal resistance when bulb brightness changes after a switch closes.

Magnetism + Induction

Big ideas

  • Magnetic fields wrap around currents according to a right-hand rule.
  • Magnetic force is always perpendicular to both velocity and magnetic field.
  • A changing magnetic flux induces an emf.
  • Lenz's law means induced effects oppose the change in flux, not the original field itself.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to reverse direction for negative charges.
  • Using $\sin\theta$ incorrectly for magnetic force or flux.
  • Confusing field direction with force direction.
  • Choosing induced current direction without first deciding whether flux is increasing or decreasing.

Practice bank

Each problem includes a hint and an answer reveal. These are adapted into a cleaner study format from the exam bank.

Resistance + Current

1. If a wire narrows while carrying the same steady current, what happens to drift speed in the narrow region?

Hint

Use $I=nqAv_d$.

Answer

It increases because the area decreases while current stays constant.

2. A resistor keeps the same material and length, but its diameter triples. How does current change for the same applied voltage?

Hint

$R \propto 1/A$ and area scales with diameter squared.

Answer

Area becomes $9A$, resistance becomes $R/9$, so current becomes 9 times larger.

3. A wire's length and diameter are both cut in half. What happens to resistance?

Hint

$R=\rho L/A$ and $A\propto d^2$.

Answer

Area becomes one fourth, length becomes one half, so $R' = \rho(L/2)/(A/4)=2R$.

4. Why is resistivity not changed when a wire is shortened or reshaped?

Hint

Ask whether resistivity is geometric or material-based.

Answer

Resistivity is a material property, so it stays the same unless the material or temperature changes.

DC Circuits + Kirchhoff

5. As more resistors are added in parallel across an ideal constant-voltage source, what happens to total source power?

Hint

Think about what happens to equivalent resistance.

Answer

Equivalent resistance decreases, so total current increases and source power increases.

6. Two wires of the same material and length are connected separately to the same voltage. One wire has twice the diameter. Which carries greater current?

Hint

Resistance depends on area.

Answer

The thicker wire carries 4 times the current because its area is 4 times larger and its resistance is 4 times smaller.

7. What is the key sign rule for a resistor when you move through it in the same direction as current?

Hint

Charges lose electric potential energy through resistors.

Answer

You write a potential drop of $-IR$.

8. Why can bulb brightness decrease when a new branch is added if the battery has internal resistance?

Hint

Consider terminal voltage when total current rises.

Answer

The larger total current causes a bigger drop across the battery's internal resistance, lowering terminal voltage available to the bulbs.

Magnetism + Induction

9. A vertical wire carries current downward. At a point east of the wire, what direction is the magnetic field?

Hint

Use the right-hand grip rule around the wire.

Answer

Toward the south.

10. An electron moves in the +x direction and feels a magnetic force in the -y direction. What is the magnetic field direction?

Hint

Work first with a positive charge, then reverse for the electron.

Answer

The field points in the $-z$ direction.

11. Does a larger magnetic flux automatically mean a larger induced emf?

Hint

Faraday's law depends on change, not just amount.

Answer

No. Induced emf depends on the rate of change of flux, not simply the size of the flux.

12. A magnetic field out of the page is increasing through a loop. What is the induced current direction?

Hint

The induced field must oppose the increase.

Answer

Clockwise, so the loop creates a field into the page.

Worked example structure

1. Identify the model

Is this microscopic current, resistor reduction, Kirchhoff analysis, magnetic force, or induction?

2. Set up symbols first

Write the governing equation before plugging in numbers. Add signs and directions clearly.

3. Check reasonableness

Ask whether the answer matches the physical trend: bigger area, smaller resistance; increasing flux, induced current opposes change.

Mixed challenge round

These are meant to feel closer to the exam. Try them without opening the hint first.

Challenge A: Equivalent Resistance

A 5.0-$\Omega$ resistor and a 9.0-$\Omega$ resistor are connected in parallel. That combination is connected in series with a 4.0-$\Omega$ resistor and a 6.0-V ideal battery. Find the current through each resistor.

Hint

Reduce the parallel branch first, then find total current, then use the branch voltage to split currents.

Solution outline

$R_p=(1/5+1/9)^{-1}=3.21\,\Omega$. Then $R_{tot}=7.21\,\Omega$, so total current is about $0.83\,A$. The 4-$\Omega$ resistor carries $0.83\,A$. Parallel branch voltage is about $2.68\,V$, so the 5-$\Omega$ branch carries about $0.53\,A$ and the 9-$\Omega$ branch carries about $0.30\,A$.

Challenge B: Magnetic Force

An electron moves at $8.0\times10^6\,m/s$ along the +x-axis into a field of 2.5 T making a 60° angle with the +x-axis in the xy-plane. Find the magnetic force magnitude and acceleration magnitude.

Hint

Use $F=qvB\sin\theta$, then $a=F/m$.

Solution outline

$F=(1.60\times10^{-19})(8.0\times10^6)(2.5)\sin60^\circ\approx2.8\times10^{-12}\,N$. Then $a=F/m_e\approx3.0\times10^{18}\,m/s^2$.

Challenge C: Solenoid Field

A solenoid has 400 turns, length 0.40 m, and current 12 A. Find the field near its center.

Hint

$n=N/L$ and $B=\mu_0 nI$.

Solution outline

$n=400/0.40=1000\,m^{-1}$, so $B=(4\pi\times10^{-7})(1000)(12)\approx1.5\times10^{-2}\,T=15\,mT$.

Challenge D: Induced EMF

A 60-turn circular coil of radius 6.0 cm is in a perpendicular magnetic field that increases from 0.20 T to 1.8 T in 0.20 s. Find the induced emf magnitude.

Hint

$\mathcal{E}=N A \dfrac{\Delta B}{\Delta t}$ for perpendicular field.

Solution outline

$A=\pi(0.060)^2$, $\Delta B=1.6\,T$, so $\mathcal{E}\approx5.4\,V$.
